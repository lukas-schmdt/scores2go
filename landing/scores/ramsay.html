Ramsay Sedation Scale

One of the first sedation scoring systems, describing six levels of sedation from anxious and agitated (Level 1) to deeply sedated with no response to stimulation (Level 6).

Ramsay et al. 1974 Popularity 72

Overview

The Ramsay Sedation Scale was introduced in 1974 by Ramsay and colleagues as one of the earliest standardised descriptions of sedation depth. It appeared in a study of controlled sedation with alphaxalone-alphadolone in 30 ICU patients, where sedation was judged satisfactory for 86% of the observation time [1]. The scale was presented as a descriptive tool in that paper and was not formally validated at publication.

The scale has six ordinal levels. Levels 1–3 describe an awake patient, from agitated to responding only to commands. Levels 4–6 describe an asleep patient, graded by the response to a light glabellar tap or a loud auditory stimulus. It gained widespread adoption in intensive care, procedural sedation and clinical trials, and remains in use in many institutions.

Current international ICU guidelines (SCCM PADIS 2018) recommend light rather than deep sedation and name the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) and the Sedation-Agitation Scale (SAS) as the preferred instruments [2]. Both were developed later with formal validation and with several defined levels of agitation [3][4].

Interpretation

The original publication did not define a target level [1]. In practice, levels 2–3 are commonly used as a light-sedation target, and some studies count level 4 as adequate as well [5]. Scores2Go presents the result in four bands:

1Agitated – inadequate sedation if sedation is intended
2–3Commonly used light-sedation target – individualise
4–5Deeper sedation – reassess whether intended
6Unresponsive – confirm this is intended; consider neurological causes

These bands summarise common practice and are not validated cut-offs. The appropriate depth depends on the clinical situation. Current guidelines favour light sedation for most mechanically ventilated adults [2].

Level 6 means only that the patient does not respond to a glabellar tap or a loud auditory stimulus. This can be intended, for example during neuromuscular blockade, burst-suppression therapy or general anaesthesia. It can also reflect a structural or metabolic brain injury rather than sedative effect. At level 1, assess for pain, delirium and other causes of agitation before deepening sedation.

Comparison to RASS

No official conversion between the Ramsay Scale and RASS exists. A small single-centre study (n = 45) found a strong correlation between the two scales and compared them at the level of sedation categories [5]:

Sedation categoryRamsayRASS
Inadequate (too light)1+1 to +4
Adequate2–40 to −3
Excessive (too deep)5–6−4 to −5

This comparison is approximate. The two scales are not interchangeable. RASS separates four levels of agitation (+1 to +4), while Ramsay groups all agitation under level 1.

Scientific Validity & Limitations

A systematic review of sedation scoring systems found that only a few of the many published instruments had been tested for reliability and validity in adult ICU patients [6]. The Ramsay Scale became the de facto reference largely because it was first, not because of a formal validation programme.

Reported reliability varies widely with the setting and the training of the raters. In the RASS validation study, trained research nurses scored the Ramsay Scale with very high interrater agreement (weighted κ 0.94). That study did not assess Ramsay's validity [7]. In a video-based study, 237 nurses rated video recordings of patients, and agreement on the Ramsay level was poor (κ 0.28) [8]. In a community-hospital ICU, non-nursing staff matched nurses' Ramsay ratings in only 29% of assessments [9].

  • One agitation level: anxiety, restlessness and dangerous agitation all fall under level 1. Agitation severity cannot be graded, unlike with RASS or SAS [3][4].
  • Mixes different dimensions: levels 1–3 describe behaviour and cooperation in awake patients, while levels 4–6 grade arousal to a physical or auditory stimulus.
  • Stimulus not standardised: "light glabellar tap" and "loud auditory stimulus" are applied differently between observers, which contributes to the variable reliability above.
  • Not usable under neuromuscular blockade: like other behaviour-based scales, it cannot assess paralysed patients.
  • Guideline position: PADIS 2018 recommends RASS or SAS for routine ICU sedation monitoring [2]. Ramsay remains useful for reading older trials and in units where it is the local standard.
