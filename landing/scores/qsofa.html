qSOFA

The Quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment is a bedside screening tool that identifies patients with suspected infection who are at greater risk of a poor outcome, without requiring laboratory testing. It is intended for use outside the ICU — in the emergency department, on general wards, or in pre-hospital settings.

Seymour et al. 2016 Popularity 80

Overview

qSOFA was introduced as part of the Sepsis-3 consensus definitions in 2016 to give clinicians a fast, bedside-only alternative to the full SOFA score for identifying patients with suspected infection who are likely to have a poor outcome [1]. Unlike SOFA, qSOFA requires no laboratory values — only respiratory rate, mental status, and blood pressure — and can be assessed in seconds.

The criteria were derived from 148,907 electronic health record encounters with suspected infection. Outside the ICU, qSOFA discriminated in-hospital mortality (AUROC 0.81) at least as well as the full SOFA score (0.79) and better than SIRS (0.76). A score of 2 or more was associated with a 3- to 14-fold increase in hospital mortality across the full range of baseline risk [2].

A positive qSOFA should prompt further investigation for organ dysfunction (for example the full SOFA score), initiation or escalation of therapy, and closer monitoring. qSOFA is a prognostic prompt, not a diagnostic criterion for sepsis — sepsis itself is defined by an acute SOFA increase of ≥ 2 points [1].

Altered mentation: Sepsis-3 and most calculators count any Glasgow Coma Scale score below 15. The original derivation model actually used GCS ≤ 13; the simpler "any alteration" definition performed no differently in sensitivity analysis [2]. Scores2Go uses the Sepsis-3 convention (GCS < 15).

Interpretation

ScoreMeaning
0–1 (negative)Does not rule out sepsis or deterioration — roughly half of patients who die score below 2. Do not defer investigation or treatment of suspected infection; reassess if clinical status changes.
≥ 2 (positive)Markedly higher risk of in-hospital death in patients with suspected infection; assess for organ dysfunction (e.g. SOFA) and consider escalation of care.

In the first prospective emergency department validation (30 EDs, 879 patients), in-hospital mortality was 3% with qSOFA < 2 versus 24% with qSOFA ≥ 2 [3]. The Sepsis-3 task force states explicitly that failing to meet 2 or more qSOFA criteria should not lead to deferral of investigation or treatment of infection [1].

Scientific Validity & Limitations

High specificity, low sensitivity. A meta-analysis of 38 studies (385,333 patients) found pooled qSOFA sensitivity for mortality of 60.8% and specificity of 72.0%, compared with 88.1% and 25.8% for SIRS; in non-ICU settings sensitivity fell to 51.2% [4]. A second meta-analysis restricted to patients outside the ICU reported a pooled sensitivity of 0.51 and specificity of 0.83 for in-hospital mortality [5]. qSOFA is therefore better at flagging high-risk patients than at reassuring about low-risk ones.

Not recommended as a single screening tool. On general wards, qSOFA (AUROC 0.69) was outperformed by the National Early Warning Score (NEWS, 0.77) for in-hospital mortality among 30,677 infected patients [6]. The 2021 Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines accordingly recommend against using qSOFA compared with SIRS, NEWS, or MEWS as a single screening tool for sepsis or septic shock (strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence) [7]. The German S3 sepsis guideline, whose 2018 version recommended regular qSOFA assessment outside the ICU, replaced this in its 2025 update with a recommendation to use a suitable screening instrument, and states that a negative qSOFA does not exclude sepsis [8].

Poor performance inside the ICU. In the derivation study qSOFA's AUROC dropped to 0.66 among ICU patients, below SOFA (0.74) [2]. In 184,875 ICU admissions from Australia and New Zealand, SOFA (crude AUROC 0.753) clearly outperformed qSOFA (0.607), which was only marginally better than SIRS (0.589) [9]. qSOFA should not be used to risk-stratify patients already in intensive care.

  • Prognostic, not diagnostic: qSOFA predicts mortality in patients with suspected infection; it neither diagnoses nor excludes sepsis.
  • Point-in-time measurement: vital signs fluctuate — a negative score should be repeated as the patient's condition evolves.
  • Confounders: pre-existing cognitive impairment, sedation, intoxication, chronic hypotension, or antihypertensive therapy can trigger criteria unrelated to infection.
