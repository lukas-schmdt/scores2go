Wells Score (PE)

Estimates the pre-test probability of pulmonary embolism in patients with suspected PE from 7 clinical criteria, guiding the choice between D-dimer testing and CT pulmonary angiography.

Overview

The Wells score for pulmonary embolism (PE) is a clinical prediction rule that converts seven bedside findings into a pre-test probability of PE. It was derived by Wells and colleagues and published in 2000, together with a strategy that combined the clinical estimate with a SimpliRED D-dimer assay [1]. A prospective emergency-department study in 2001 then showed that patients with a low clinical probability and a negative D-dimer could safely be managed without diagnostic imaging [2]. A large prospective management study later confirmed that an algorithm combining the dichotomized Wells score, D-dimer testing, and CT was a safe and effective way to manage suspected PE [3].

The score is only meant for patients in whom PE is clinically suspected. It is not a screening tool for asymptomatic patients, and it must never delay resuscitation: haemodynamically unstable patients with suspected PE follow a separate, immediate-imaging pathway.

Besides the original three-tier and the two-tier (“PE unlikely” / “PE likely”) interpretations, a simplified version with one point per item (Gibson 2008; ≤ 1 point = PE unlikely) has been proposed [4]. Scores2Go implements the original weighted version only. The Wells criteria have been prospectively validated in emergency-department populations [5], and they are commonly used alongside the PERC rule for patients with very low pre-test probability [6].

Interpretation

The score can be interpreted in two ways. Current guidelines accept either version; the two-tier version maps most directly onto the D-dimer/CTPA pathway (see below).

Wells-based strategies can be combined with or compared to other approaches. The YEARS algorithm uses three clinical items together with a variable D-dimer threshold and was shown to reduce the number of CT scans needed while remaining safe [8]. The 2019 ESC guidelines recommend assessing clinical probability with a validated rule such as the Wells score or the revised Geneva score, and combining it with D-dimer testing in patients with low or intermediate probability (or “PE unlikely”) [9].

Scientific Validity & Limitations

The Wells score has been validated repeatedly in emergency-department and mixed populations and is embedded in international guidelines [3][5][9]. It is nevertheless a probability estimate, not a diagnosis, and has well-recognised limitations:

  • Subjective item: “PE is the #1 diagnosis or equally likely” carries 3 points and depends on the clinician’s gestalt. Inter-rater reliability for this item is variable, so different clinicians can reach different categories for the same patient.
  • Only for suspected PE: the score assumes that PE is already a clinical consideration. Applying it to unselected patients is not appropriate.
  • Unstable patients: haemodynamically unstable patients follow a different pathway; resuscitation and immediate imaging or bedside echocardiography should never wait for a score.
  • Prevalence varies: the prevalence attached to each category differs by setting, era and imaging technology, so the percentages shown are indicative only.
  • D-dimer limitation: in the “PE likely” group, a negative D-dimer is not considered sufficient to exclude PE.
  • Simplified variant: the Gibson simplified rule (1 point per item, ≤ 1 = unlikely) was proposed to reduce subjectivity and complexity [4]; it is not implemented here.

Related tools include the PERC rule, which can identify patients at very low risk in whom no testing is needed [6]; the age-adjusted D-dimer cutoff validated in the ADJUST-PE study [7]; the YEARS algorithm [8]; and the revised Geneva score, an alternative validated clinical probability rule [9]. When the leg is the main concern, the companion Wells Score (DVT) estimates the probability of deep vein thrombosis.
