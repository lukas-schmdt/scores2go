RASS

The Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale assesses sedation depth and agitation in ICU patients. It provides a standardised 10-point scale ranging from +4 (combative) to −5 (unarousable).

Sessler et al. 2002 Popularity 90

Overview

The Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) measures a patient's level of agitation or sedation in the ICU on a single 10-level scale from +4 (combative) to −5 (unarousable), with 0 meaning alert and calm. Positive values describe agitation, negative values sedation. In the derivation study, inter-rater reliability among two physicians, two nurses and one pharmacist was excellent (r = 0.956; κ = 0.73) and held across medical, surgical, cardiac surgery, coronary and neuroscience ICUs; RASS correlated highly with a visual analogue scale of sedation (r = 0.93) [1].

A subsequent prospective cohort study in medical and coronary ICUs (275 ventilated patients for validity testing) confirmed excellent inter-rater reliability (weighted κ = 0.91). RASS was the first sedation scale validated for detecting changes in sedation status over consecutive ICU days, and it correlated with the administered doses of sedatives and analgesics [2]. The 2013 SCCM PAD guidelines recommend RASS and the Sedation-Agitation Scale (SAS) among the available sedation scales [3], and the two are regarded as the most validated and reliable sedation scales in ICU use [4].

RASS is routinely paired with delirium screening tools such as the CAM-ICU, which was validated for mechanically ventilated patients [5] and includes an altered level of consciousness as one of its four features [3].

Assessment Procedure

  1. Observe the patient. If alert and calm, score 0. If the patient is restless, agitated, or combative, score +1 to +4.
  2. If not alert, call the patient by name and ask them to open their eyes and look at you (repeat once if needed). Sustained awakening with eye contact (> 10 s): −1. Brief awakening with eye contact (< 10 s): −2. Any movement to voice but no eye contact: −3.
  3. If there is no response to voice, stimulate physically by shaking the shoulder and/or rubbing the sternum. Any movement to physical stimulation: −4. No response: −5.

The assessment relies on the duration of eye contact after verbal stimulation and takes less than 20 seconds to perform [3].

Interpretation & Sedation Targets

The SCCM PADIS 2018 guideline makes a conditional recommendation for light rather than deep sedation in critically ill, mechanically ventilated adults [6][4]. There is, however, no defined RASS cutoff for light sedation, which limits comparison between studies [4]: studies have, for example, defined light sedation as RASS −2 to 0 [3] and a large trial comparing dexmedetomidine with usual sedation (3,912 patients) targeted RASS −2 to +1 [7]. The target should therefore be set individually for each patient.

Deep sedation is not harmless: in a multicentre cohort from 25 Australian and New Zealand hospitals, early deep sedation (RASS −3 to −5 in the first 48 hours) independently predicted delayed extubation and higher hospital and 180-day mortality [8].

ABCDEF Bundle

Sedation monitoring with RASS is part of the C component ("Choice of analgesia and sedation") of the ICU Liberation ABCDEF Bundle, an evidence-based guide for coordinating ICU patient care [3]:

  • A — Assess, prevent, and manage pain
  • B — Both spontaneous awakening and spontaneous breathing trials
  • C — Choice of analgesia and sedation
  • D — Delirium (e.g. CAM-ICU / ICDSC)
  • E — Early mobility
  • F — Family engagement

Scientific Validity & Limitations

RASS and the SAS [9] are considered the two most validated and reliable ICU sedation scales [4]. RASS correlated with both the Ramsay scale (r = −0.78) and the SAS (r = 0.78) in its derivation study [1], and RASS and Ramsay both showed excellent inter-rater reliability (weighted κ 0.91 and 0.94), superior to the Glasgow Coma Scale (0.64) [2]. Unlike the Ramsay scale, which has a single agitation category, RASS grades agitation in four levels [3][9].

Known limitations:

  • Requires a motor or eye response: every level is defined by the patient's movement, eye opening or eye contact, so RASS cannot grade sedation depth in patients under neuromuscular blockade.
  • Not a pain or delirium measure: RASS describes arousal only. Pain should be assessed separately (BPS and CPOT are the most valid behavioural pain scales for patients unable to communicate), and delirium with CAM-ICU or ICDSC [3].
  • No agreed definition of light sedation: studies use different RASS cutoffs, which limits comparison of sedation targets and outcomes across studies [4].
  • Inter-rater agreement is high, not perfect: κ ranged from 0.64 to 0.82 across patient subgroups in the derivation study [1], and each score is a single bedside observation of a state that can change over time.
